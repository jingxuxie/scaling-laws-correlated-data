\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Empirical and functional scaling laws.}
Early large-scale studies reported predictable power-law learning curves and
sublinear growth of the model size needed to exploit additional data
\cite{hestness2017deep}.  Subsequent work proposed joint functional forms for
model and dataset scaling \cite{rosenfeld2020constructive}, established
parameter--data--compute laws for language modeling
\cite{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022training}, and documented transitions that
require smoothly broken rather than single power laws
\cite{caballero2023broken}.  These papers treat scaling primarily as an
empirical regularity to be fitted and extrapolated.  Our aim is complementary:
we construct two data streams with the same marginal task and identify a
specific temporal mechanism that changes the exponent itself.

\paragraph{Spectral learning curves.}
Learning curves for Gaussian processes and kernel regression have long been
expressed through covariance-operator eigenvalues
\cite{sollich1998learning,bordelon2020spectrum}.  Task--kernel alignment further
shows that generalization depends on how target energy is distributed across
those eigenfunctions \cite{canatar2021spectral}.  Solvable random-feature and
statistical-mechanics models connect power-law spectra to neural scaling
\cite{maloney2022solvable,bahri2024explaining}, while feature learning can
change optimization and compute exponents beyond the fixed-kernel regime
\cite{bordelon2024feature}.  These analyses share the idea that a sample budget
resolves progressively weaker modes.  We add a second spectral object: the
innovation law $q_j\propto\lambda_j/\tau_j$, which determines how quickly each
mode receives statistically new observations.

\paragraph{Scaling laws in linear regression.}
Our closest starting point is the sketched infinite-dimensional linear model
of \citet{lin2024scaling}, which derives joint model, data, and compute laws for
one-pass SGD under a power-law covariance spectrum.  \citet{lin2025reuse}
show that multi-pass reuse can improve the iteration term under a source
condition.  Recent extensions study mini-batch sampling protocols
\cite{chen2026minibatch} and optimizer-specific effects such as signSGD
\cite{kim2026signsgd}.  In all of these works, the distinctive resource is
optimization over a fixed collection of examples drawn from the same marginal
law.  Our bottleneck is orthogonal: repeated optimization can reduce error on
observed directions but cannot identify a direction that has not refreshed.
The resulting data term is a target-weighted coverage law.

Rare or sparse features provide another route to coverage-limited scaling.
\citet{sous2026sparse} study sparse activations and show that unobserved
coordinates can induce asymmetric exponents and double descent.  Their
sparsity changes which features appear in an individual input.  We instead
hold the one-time covariance distribution fixed across persistence profiles
and change only temporal organization.  This lets us compare streams with the
same marginal spectrum, and even the same global integrated autocorrelation,
while obtaining different learning slopes.

\paragraph{Learning from dependent observations.}
Classical and modern analyses often quantify dependence through a scalar
mixing time, spectral gap, or variance-inflation factor.  Examples include
concentration for Markov chains \cite{fan2021hoeffding}, finite-time SGD under
Markov randomness \cite{doan2020finite}, and least squares from a single
trajectory \cite{nagaraj2020markovian,sun2025single}.  Generalization bounds
have also been developed through delayed or Wasserstein-stability
online-to-batch reductions
\cite{abeles2025mixing,chatterjee2025dependent}, while consistency results for
kernel methods permit broad non-i.i.d. processes
\cite{massiani2024dependent}.  These theories provide guarantees in terms of
global process descriptors; they do not ask how dependence aligned with an
infinite predictive spectrum changes a scaling exponent.

Sharp high-dimensional regression results address a different but related
limit.  \citet{atanasov2025correlated} analyze ridge regression with a shared
sample-correlation operator and derive corrected risk and cross-validation
formulas.  \citet{moniri2025dependent} prove Gaussian universality for broad
linearly dependent designs in a proportional regime.  Their spatio-temporal
models are dense: each observation contains all coordinates, and dependence
primarily changes sample-covariance fluctuations.  Our exact theorem concerns
innovation coverage, where different predictive directions refresh at
different rates.  The dense Gaussian autoregressive experiment reported below is included
precisely to show that these settings need not share an exponent.

\paragraph{Occupancy, renewal, and effective sample size.}
At refresh times, spectral renewal regression is an infinite-urn scheme with
frequencies $q_j$.  Occupancy and missing-mass asymptotics under regular
variation originate in work such as \citet{karlin1968urn} and are developed in
modern form by \citet{gnedin2007occupancy} and
\citet{benhamou2017concentration}.  Our risk is a \emph{target-weighted}
missing mass: an unseen mode has cost $s_j$, whose exponent can differ from the
occupancy frequencies.  At raw time horizons, size-biased dwell lengths
produce the renewal inspection paradox.  Our noisy transfer uses the
fractional-moment inequality of \citet{vonbahr1965inequalities}.  Finally, the
matched-autocorrelation construction uses integrated autocorrelation time in
the sense of Markov-chain variance inflation \cite{geyer1992practical}, but
shows that averaging over modes can erase the alignment information needed to
predict the learning curve.
