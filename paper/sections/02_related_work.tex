\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Spectral scaling laws.}
Empirical neural scaling laws were documented by \citet{kaplan2020scaling} and
refined for compute-optimal training by \citet{hoffmann2022training}.
Eigenvalue-based theories connect learning curves to spectral resolution
\cite{bordelon2020spectrum,maloney2022solvable,bahri2024explaining}; feature
learning can alter these rates beyond the kernel regime
\cite{bordelon2024feature}. Our closest starting point is the
infinite-dimensional sketched linear model of \citet{lin2024scaling}, which
derives parameter--data laws for one-pass SGD. \citet{lin2025reuse} studies
multi-pass reuse. We instead hold the marginal task fixed and change the rate
at which distinct spectral modes arrive. Repeating a persistent block can
reduce optimization error but cannot reveal an absent mode.

\paragraph{Regression with dependent samples.}
Markovian least squares and stochastic optimization are commonly characterized
through global mixing quantities
\cite{nagaraj2020markovian,doan2020finite,sun2025single}. In proportional
high-dimensional limits, \citet{atanasov2025correlated} derive sharp ridge
formulas for matrix-Gaussian samples with a shared sample-correlation operator,
while \citet{moniri2025dependent} establish Gaussian universality for broad
linearly dependent processes. Our question is different: feature direction
$j$ has its own refresh time $\tau_j$, and the alignment of this temporal
hierarchy with the marginal spectrum is what changes the exponent.

\paragraph{Occupancy and renewal theory.}
At innovation times, the problem is an infinite-urn scheme with frequencies
$q_j$. Missing-mass asymptotics under regular variation are classical
\cite{gnedin2007occupancy,benhamou2017concentration}. Our loss is a
\emph{target-weighted} missing mass: unseen mode $j$ costs $s_j$, whose exponent
need not match $q_j$. For raw horizons, heavy dwell times introduce the renewal
inspection paradox. Our noisy transfer uses the fractional-moment inequality
of \citet{vonbahr1965inequalities}, avoiding an unnecessary finite-variance
assumption.
