\section{Introduction}

Scaling laws summarize how predictive loss decreases as models, data, and
compute grow. They have become an empirical planning tool for large language
models \cite{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022training} and a theoretical target
for random features, kernels, and linear regression
\cite{bahri2024explaining,maloney2022solvable,bordelon2020spectrum,lin2024scaling}.
A recurring spectral picture is that larger datasets resolve progressively
weaker eigenmodes of the data distribution. With covariance spectrum
$\lambda_j\asymp j^{-a}$ and target energy $s_j\asymp j^{-b}$, the largest
learnable index is typically a power of the sample size; summing the unresolved
target tail then produces a power-law learning curve.

Most scaling-law theories assume independent samples. Yet many practically
important datasets are trajectories: financial and physical time series,
robot experience, logged interactions, and replay buffers. A common response
is to replace the nominal sample size $N$ by a scalar effective sample size.
This is appropriate when every feature direction shares a common correlation
profile. It becomes inadequate when weak spectral directions are also the
slowest directions to change. In that case there is no single rate at which
new information arrives.

This paper studies the following question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{When does sample dependence change a scaling exponent rather than only
a constant?}
\end{quote}

We answer it in an exactly solvable persistent regression model. Mode $j$
has test probability $\lambda_j$ but persists for time $\tau_j$. To preserve
the same one-time marginal distribution, a new block selects mode $j$ with
probability
\begin{equation}
q_j
=\frac{\lambda_j/\tau_j}{\sum_{\ell\ge1}\lambda_\ell/\tau_\ell}.
\label{eq:innovation-law-intro}
\end{equation}
Thus the marginal spectrum is $\lambda$, whereas the spectrum of
statistically new arrivals is $q$. Under power laws, $q_j\asymp
j^{-(a+r)}$, producing the learnable resolution
\begin{equation}
J_B\asymp B^{1/(a+r)}.
\label{eq:resolution-intro}
\end{equation}
The target energy beyond $J_B$ is
$\sum_{j>J_B}s_j\asymp B^{-(b-1)/(a+r)}$. This is the central mechanism.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item We define spectral renewal regression, in which different
    persistence profiles have the same one-time covariance but different
    innovation spectra.
    \item We derive an exact finite-$B$ risk decomposition into model
    approximation, unseen-mode bias, and label-noise variance.
    \item Under power-law covariance, persistence, and source conditions, we
    prove matching rates in model size and data. In the noiseless case we
    prove an exact minimax identity, showing that the changed exponent is an
    information limitation rather than an artifact of a particular optimizer.
    \item We transfer the noiseless rate from a fixed number of innovations
    to a fixed raw trajectory length under finite-variance dwell times.
    \item We construct stationary semi-Markov and reversible Markov processes
    with the required mode-wise autocorrelation, derive an exact scaling
    function at the model--data frontier, and verify all predicted slopes with
    lightweight reproducible experiments.
\end{enumerate}

The scope is deliberate. Our theorem concerns persistent spectral sampling;
it does not assert that every dense Gaussian autoregression has the same
exponent. The model is useful because it cleanly separates marginal spectral
mass from the rate at which independent spectral information appears.
