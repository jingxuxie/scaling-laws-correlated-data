\section{Introduction}

Scaling laws turn small experiments into forecasts of how loss changes with
model size, data, and compute. They are now central to resource planning in
large-scale learning \cite{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022training}, while
spectral theories explain power laws through the progressive resolution of
weaker covariance or kernel modes
\cite{bordelon2020spectrum,bahri2024explaining,lin2024scaling}. Nearly all such
theories begin with independent examples. Real datasets, however, are often
contiguous trajectories: robot logs, sensor streams, financial histories, and
replay buffers.

A standard correction replaces the nominal sample count by a scalar effective
sample size. This is sensible when every feature direction shares one temporal
kernel. It is incomplete when the weak directions that dominate late-stage
learning are also the slowest to refresh. Then information arrives at a
\emph{spectrum} of rates. We ask:
\begin{quote}
\emph{When does dependence change a scaling exponent, rather than only a
constant?}
\end{quote}

We isolate the mechanism in an exactly solvable model. Mode $j$ occupies a
fraction $\lambda_j$ of raw time but lasts $\tau_j$ steps whenever it appears.
Preserving the same one-time covariance forces new blocks to choose mode $j$
with probability
\begin{equation}
q_j=\frac{\lambda_j/\tau_j}{\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell/\tau_\ell}.
\label{eq:intro-q}
\end{equation}
Thus the marginal spectrum is $\lambda$, whereas the \emph{innovation
spectrum} is $q$. If $\lambda_j\asymp j^{-a}$ and
$\tau_j\asymp j^r$, then $q_j\asymp j^{-(a+r)}$. After $B$ innovations, the
largest mode seen with constant probability is
$J_B\asymp B^{1/(a+r)}$; unresolved target energy
$s_j\asymp j^{-b}$ therefore contributes
$B^{-(b-1)/(a+r)}$.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We define \emph{spectral renewal regression} and derive an exact
finite-sample bias--variance identity. Under power laws, it yields matching
model, innovation, and noise terms.
\item We prove an exact noiseless minimax equality: no optimizer or data-reuse
strategy can identify the sign of a mode that never arrived.
\item We pass from innovations to exactly $N$ raw examples without a
finite-variance assumption. Boundary initialization retains the innovation
exponent for every $r\ge0$, while equilibrium initialization can enter a new
inspection-paradox phase.
\item Using the von Bahr--Esseen inequality, we obtain a noisy raw-horizon rate
under fractional, rather than second, dwell moments. This covers substantial
infinite-variance regimes.
\item We show that the theorem is invariant under known well-conditioned dense
representations. Synthetic and real-data tests verify the predicted regimes
and, importantly, show that dense autoregressive correlation without an
innovation bottleneck need not obey the $a+r$ law.
\end{enumerate}

The last point delimits the claim: autocorrelation by itself is not enough. The
exponent changes when dependence throttles the arrival of new predictive
spectral information.
